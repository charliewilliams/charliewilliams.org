## The problem

Shazam did something that felt like magic: point a phone at a speaker and it tells you what is playing. Underneath, it was an audio fingerprinting engine — probabilistic, invisible, and completely opaque to the person holding the phone.

The engineering problem on the app side was not the recognition. It was everything around it. A result that arrives in two seconds feels like magic; the same result at six seconds feels broken. A confident wrong answer is worse than an honest failure. None of that is solved by the algorithm.

## What I did

I was one of five engineers on the iOS team, building and maintaining the iPhone and iPad apps — the consumer surface sitting on top of the recognition engine.

- Shipped and maintained a top-tier consumer app at very large scale, through Apple's review process and the constraints of the era's devices and networks.
- Worked on the parts that determine whether the technology feels trustworthy: latency, feedback, failure states, and what the app does when it is not sure.

## The outcome

Shazam reached over 250 million people and was later acquired by Apple.

I bring it up now for a specific reason. A great many companies currently have a model and no idea how to put a product around it — how to make something probabilistic feel dependable to someone who does not care how it works. That is the same problem, ten years earlier.
